# IdleCommand — Causal World & Story Engine Plan

## Purpose

This plan turns the Nordic Neolithic camp into the foundation of a world that can grow for years without becoming a conventional city builder.

The central promise is simple:

> Nothing important simply appears. Every meaningful object, place, skill, and change has an observable history.

The player should be able to look at a hide rack, a basket, a path, a shelter, or eventually an entire town and understand that somebody discovered a need, found material, did the work, encountered difficulty, and left a trace.

This is an autonomous storytelling system. The player witnesses and remembers. Nora, Otto, Milo, and later inhabitants decide, explore, learn, cooperate, fail, recover, and build.

## 1. Experience target

### What the player should feel

- “Something happened while I was away.”
- “That object is there for a reason.”
- “I remember when they found that place.”
- “The camp has habits, but it is not repeating a script.”
- “The world is larger now because they became curious about it.”
- “This settlement has a past.”

### What the player should never feel

- that assets pop into existence after an invisible counter completes
- that people are worker units awaiting orders
- that the scene is a production dashboard disguised as nature
- that unexplored land is merely a locked content panel
- that progress erases the intimacy of the original camp
- that missing a moment causes punishment or permanent loss

## 2. Non-negotiable causal laws

### Law 1 — Every artifact has provenance

Every significant artifact records who conceived it, why it was needed, where its materials originated, who transformed them, which tools and places were used, what changed during construction, and when it entered the world.

This applies equally to a tinder bundle, a drying rack, a house beam, a market stall, and a future church bell.

### Law 2 — Every creation is visible in stages

Construction and transformation are represented by physical intermediate states. A drying rack is first poles on the ground, then supports, then a crossbar, then cordage, then a hanging hide. A shelter is stakes, frame, woven covering, repair, and wear.

The game may compress time, but it may not skip the legible story.

### Law 3 — Interactive nature is a world object

The distant forest and mountains may remain painted background scenery. Anything that can be touched, gathered, felled, depleted, regrown, remembered, or revisited must be a separate simulation asset: foreground trees, berry bushes, hazel stands, reed beds, flint outcrops, clay banks, animal tracks, fallen trunks, and water edges.

### Law 4 — Discovery precedes exploitation

Characters cannot use a location or resource merely because it exists in data. Someone must notice it, approach it, inspect it, remember it, and return with that knowledge. Discovery is a small story beat, not a UI unlock.

### Law 5 — Knowledge is carried by people

A settlement does not own abstract technology. Individuals know techniques, teach them, imitate them, improve them, and sometimes forget details across generations. Knowledge has social provenance just as objects have material provenance.

### Law 6 — Change leaves traces

A felled tree leaves a stump, chips, a changed silhouette, and later regrowth. Repeated walking creates a path. A failed fire leaves wet ash. A dismantled shelter leaves post holes or reused poles. Growth adds history rather than replacing it.

### Law 7 — Failure produces story, not punishment

Wet tinder, an empty hunt, a cracked vessel, spoiled berries, or an awkwardly fallen tree create believable responses and future decisions. They do not generate alerts, guilt, or a demand that the player intervene.

## 3. Day One: beginning with almost nothing

The eventual canonical opening begins before the familiar camp exists. Nora, Otto, and Milo arrive with only the clothes and small personal items visible on their bodies, embodied knowledge, one another, and the immediate landscape.

They do not begin with a completed shelter, lit fire, drying rack, basket, pottery, stored wood, or convenient material piles.

The first days should form a compact, readable origin story:

1. They pause and assess the place.
2. Milo investigates scent and water.
3. One person identifies a sheltered patch of ground.
4. They collect loose tinder and kindling by hand.
5. They discover usable flint and pyrite, or bring a tiny personal fire-making set if historical pacing requires it.
6. They attempt the first fire; dampness or wind may complicate it.
7. The first flame changes posture, grouping, light, and mood.
8. They choose nearby poles and reeds or hides for a primitive shelter.
9. The shelter appears through visible construction phases.
10. Repeated trips begin to mark the first path.

No survival bar is shown. The world communicates need through posture, weather response, search behaviour, warmth-seeking, conversation, and choice.

## 4. Core causal chains

These chains are not quests. They are networks of opportunities, prerequisites, decisions, and consequences that the autonomous planner can combine differently.

### 4.1 First fire

Possible chain:

1. Notice that evening is cooling.
2. Search for a dry location.
3. Find dry grass, bark, fungus, or fine fibres.
4. Gather fallen twigs or break dead lower branches.
5. Locate or remember flint and pyrite.
6. Prepare a tinder nest.
7. Strike sparks.
8. Protect the ember from wind.
9. Blow gently.
10. Add fine kindling.
11. Add larger branches only after the flame is stable.
12. Sit near it, dry clothing, cook, talk, or sleep.

Visible failure branches include wet tinder that only smokes, a gust scattering fibres, heavy kindling smothering an ember, rain reducing the fire to steam, and cold ash that must be rebuilt in the morning.

The fire remembers its own history through ash depth, charcoal, stone darkening, nearby wood, and habitual seating positions.

### 4.2 Tree to shelter, fuel, and rack

Foreground trees have species, age, size, condition, accessible branches, and material affordances.

Possible chain:

1. Discover a suitable tree or stand.
2. Assess whether dead branches are sufficient.
3. Gather fallen material first.
4. If a pole is required, make or retrieve a stone axe.
5. Select a young straight tree rather than randomly attacking scenery.
6. Chop in repeated, restrained animation beats.
7. Change the tree from intact to notched to leaning to fallen.
8. Create separate trunk, branches, chips, leaves, and stump objects.
9. Remove branches.
10. Drag or carry pieces according to size.
11. Split material into poles, handles, firewood, pegs, or supports.
12. Store it physically at a chosen place.
13. Use it in a visible build sequence.

The world later shows the consequences: fewer nearby straight poles, a mossy stump, a wider path to the grove, and a new sapling growing nearby.

### 4.3 Berry discovery and food practice

Possible chain:

1. An explorer notices colour, scent, birds, or animal interest near a bush.
2. They inspect the plant rather than instantly harvesting it.
3. Personal knowledge determines confidence.
4. They taste cautiously or recognize it from memory.
5. They carry a handful home.
6. The second trip creates a reason to make a container.
7. Flexible hazel or willow rods are gathered.
8. A small basket is woven over time.
9. Later harvests become more efficient and visibly larger.
10. The berry patch changes with season and previous harvesting.

Possible stories include Milo finding the patch first, Nora teaching Otto, birds consuming part of the crop, or a basket breaking and being repaired.

### 4.4 Hunt to hide rack

Hunting must never be represented by a hide appearing after a character walks offscreen.

Possible chain:

1. Tracks, droppings, movement, or Milo's behaviour reveal animal presence.
2. A character observes direction, freshness, weather, and daylight.
3. They prepare an appropriate tool.
4. The hunt crosses a known or newly revealed location.
5. The outcome is uncertain: successful, empty-handed, delayed, injured, or abandoned.
6. A successful return includes a physically carried carcass or staged processing at the kill site.
7. Butchering creates meat, hide, bone, sinew, and waste as distinct materials.
8. Preservation creates a new need.
9. Straight poles are sourced and shaped.
10. Cordage is made from fibre or sinew.
11. The drying frame is assembled in visible stages.
12. The hide is scraped, stretched, turned, dried, smoked, softened, or repaired over multiple sessions.

The rack therefore becomes a condensed story of exploration, risk, material knowledge, cooperation, and time.

### 4.5 Clay to vessel

Possible chain:

1. Wet footprints or an eroded bank expose workable clay.
2. Someone tests its texture.
3. Clay is carried home as a visible lump.
4. Temper is gathered and mixed.
5. The vessel is coiled and shaped.
6. It dries in a sheltered place.
7. A suitable firing arrangement is prepared.
8. Firing may succeed, crack, discolour, or deform it.
9. The vessel gains a use and a repair or breakage history.

This chain enters the active prototype only after fire, wood, plant gathering, and shelter provenance are convincing.

## 5. World object model

Every interactive object uses the same conceptual contract.

### Identity

- stable world ID
- object family and species or material
- position, orientation, and current location
- creation or discovery event ID
- parent objects and source location
- current owner, carrier, or shared-place association

### Physical state

- form: tree, trunk, pole, chips, basket, hide, ash, and so on
- condition: dry, damp, fresh, worn, cracked, blunt, rotten
- quantity represented by visible discrete units where practical
- weight and carry mode
- stage of growth, depletion, processing, or construction
- exposed affordances: gather, inspect, cut, drag, dry, burn, repair

### Social and historical state

- who knows about it
- who last handled it
- personal or communal meaning
- important events associated with it
- whether it is part of a remembered story
- whether its appearance is familiar enough for lower-detail simulation

### Environmental state

- wetness and drying rate
- temperature and proximity to fire
- seasonal availability
- regrowth or decay
- shelter and sunlight exposure
- local traffic and disturbance

## 6. Material lineage: the provenance graph

The simulation keeps an internal directed graph of transformations.

Examples:

`young pine #17 → felled trunk #31 → pole #44 → east support of hide rack #52`

`deer #8 → hide #61 → scraped hide #67 → winter cloak #103`

The graph creates story hooks: Otto can recognize a cracked rack pole as the tree he felled in the first winter; Nora can repair a basket woven by someone no longer present; a future market stall can use beams reclaimed from the original longhouse.

The player never needs to see the graph as a technical interface. It is expressed through remembered comments, visible wear, placement, and the optional Chronicle.

## 7. Action and transformation model

Every autonomous action is authored as a staged possibility rather than a single animation plus reward.

An action definition contains:

- goal and emotional motive
- valid actors and relevant traits
- required knowledge
- required source objects and conditions
- required tools or work locations
- weather, daylight, and season preferences
- approach and body-position anchors
- facing and hand-contact anchors
- visible stages and animation variants
- interruption-safe checkpoints
- outputs and provenance links
- failure possibilities
- memories and relationship effects
- cooldown and repetition cost

Example: preparing tinder means walking to a dry-grass patch, kneeling at its interaction anchor, pulling fibres, standing with a visible bundle, carrying it to a dry place, placing it, and shaping it into a nest. If rain interrupts the action, the bundle stays where it was placed and becomes wet instead of vanishing into an inventory.

## 8. Autonomous story planner

The engine should not select actions from a fixed daily loop. It evaluates intentions in context.

### Inputs

- physical comfort without punitive survival pressure
- time, light, weather, and season
- nearby opportunities and known distant places
- unfinished work
- available materials and tools
- character traits, knowledge, energy, mood, and curiosity
- relationships and recent shared experiences
- Milo's attachment, scent interests, and discoveries
- novelty and repetition history
- remembered failures and successes

### Candidate intentions

Examples include seeking warmth, maintaining the fire, investigating a scent, revisiting berries, gathering a missing material, improving shelter before rain, repairing an object, teaching, inviting someone to rest, playing with Milo, continuing construction, or abandoning a plan because conditions changed.

### Selection

Each candidate receives a contextual score. The system reduces recently repeated actions and raises opportunities that create readable contrast or shared moments. Randomness chooses among plausible options; it does not choose implausible behaviour.

### Coordination

Some intentions invite an initiator, helper, observer or learner, companion, and dog involvement. Cooperation can be accepted, refused, delayed, or continued alone based on relationships and mood.

### Interruptions

Actions define safe interruption points. Rain may interrupt berries, a fading fire may interrupt rest, and Milo may redirect an explorer toward tracks. Materials and construction stages remain in the world so the story can resume naturally.

## 9. Story grammar and variation

The storytelling engine combines four layers:

1. **Need or opportunity** — cold evening, fresh tracks, ripe berries, loose shelter covering.
2. **Character interpretation** — curiosity, caution, confidence, habit, memory, relationship.
3. **Physical process** — movement, contact, transformation, transport, construction.
4. **Consequence and trace** — warm gathering, empty return, new path, broken tool, shared memory.

Variation comes from context, not cosmetic random lines. The same berry bush produces different stories because of season, weather, who discovers it, who joins, whether a basket exists, Milo's presence, and what the food is later used for.

To prevent mechanical repetition:

- maintain per-character and global recent-action memory
- prefer unfinished causal chains over disconnected busywork
- vary approach direction, posture, tempo, partner, and aftermath
- allow quiet gaps where nobody performs a content action
- make social responses optional and context-sensitive
- avoid repeating whisper text for visually obvious events

## 10. Spatial world architecture

### 10.1 Three visual layers

**Scenic depth:** painted mountains, distant forest masses, sky, and inaccessible far water establish atmosphere. They are not harvestable.

**Living foreground:** trees, bushes, stones, reeds, clay, tracks, paths, structures, people, and animals are separate assets with world state and interaction anchors.

**Unknown horizon:** unvisited neighbouring areas exist as low-detail terrain possibilities. They are not shown as padlocks or fog-of-war tiles. Their boundaries are hidden naturally by composition, haze, trees, ridges, and camera framing.

### 10.2 Exploration and map growth

The map does not expand because an XP threshold is reached. It becomes known because a character travels beyond the familiar place and returns with knowledge.

Sequence:

1. A motive creates an exploration intention.
2. The character leaves along a believable route.
3. The camera may gently follow only if the moment is readable and non-disruptive.
4. A new place is encountered and receives a stable identity.
5. The explorer observes, samples, marks, or remembers it.
6. Knowledge returns to camp socially.
7. Future travel reinforces a path.
8. The overview camera eases outward enough to include the new known place.

No location pops into a menu. It first exists as geography and experience.

### 10.3 Expanding camera bounds

The desktop composition begins as one intimate strip. As the known world widens, the camera keeps the main camp dominant, reveals only meaningful routes and places, eases zoom over several seconds, retains a readable sprite scale, and simplifies distant detail atmospherically.

The player may later choose a closer view, but the world continues autonomously at every zoom.

### 10.4 View hierarchy

- **World strip:** the whole known local world at a calm, low-detail scale. Large movement, weather, smoke, lights, and travel remain readable.
- **Place view:** a camp, grove, river crossing, field, market, garden, or town district. Most everyday storytelling occurs here.
- **Intimate view:** a specific process such as knapping flint, lighting a fire, weaving, teaching, or repair.

These are spatial scales of one continuous world, not separate management screens.

### 10.5 Later settlement locations

Future locations such as a church, square, market, house, workshop, and garden use the same place model as the first campfire: physical approaches, inhabitants, habitual visitors, visible work, construction provenance, memories, and exterior signs of interior life.

The player zooms toward them because they are places in the world, not buttons on a city interface.

## 11. Simulation scale and level of detail

The world can grow without simulating every distant hand motion every frame.

### Near camera

- exact movement and contact anchors
- staged animation
- individual carried objects
- particles, sound, and small reactions

### Known but distant

- complete causal decisions and event timing
- simplified movement paths
- aggregated visual groups when necessary
- preserved provenance and memory

### Off camera

- deterministic progression at coarse time steps
- interruption and failure rules still applied
- important events recorded for visible aftermath
- characters return with the correct physical objects, condition, and memories

When the player zooms back in, the engine reconstructs state from real history rather than inventing a decorative approximation.

## 12. Settlement growth without pop-in

Every permanent structure follows a visual lifecycle:

1. a need becomes socially present
2. a location is considered and prepared
3. materials are sourced and staged nearby
4. foundations or post holes appear
5. the frame rises in readable increments
6. covering, walls, roof, or fittings are added
7. inhabitants use incomplete parts when plausible
8. completion is marked by behaviour, not a reward banner
9. weathering, repair, adaptation, and reuse continue

Traffic creates paths. Waste creates middens. Gardens alter soil. Smoke stains roofs. Repeated repair changes silhouettes. Growth makes the settlement denser in history, not merely denser in assets.

## 13. Ages and technological change

An age is not a themed content switch. It is the accumulated result of materials, knowledge, population, environment, trade, belief, and memory.

Technological change requires a motive, material access, a knowledgeable or experimenting person, visible attempts, and teaching before broad adoption. Older techniques and objects persist.

Old structures are repaired, reused, abandoned, buried, commemorated, or built around. The first camp should remain archaeologically and emotionally present beneath the future town.

## 14. Persistence and the Chronicle

The save format should eventually contain an event ledger in addition to current state.

Each event records its identity, time, characters, objects, location, intention, trigger, stages, outcome, transformations, memories, and visible traces.

Most events remain quiet history. A small subset enters the Chronicle because it is a first, a meaningful failure or recovery, relationally important, linked to a long-lived artifact, a discovery, or a moment the player observed closely.

The Chronicle describes genuine simulated history. It never fabricates drama disconnected from the world.

## 15. Content authoring structure

Recommended data-driven definitions:

- `WorldObjectDefinition`: material, states, affordances, visual stages
- `ActionDefinition`: roles, anchors, stages, timing, requirements, outputs
- `PlaceDefinition`: spatial affordances, routes, discovery cues, atmosphere
- `KnowledgeDefinition`: techniques, observation sources, teaching paths
- `EventDefinition`: rare contextual possibilities and aftermath
- `CharacterProfile`: traits, preferences, knowledge, relationships, animation identity

Every authored action includes a visual contact test, interruption test, provenance test, repetition test, and desktop-glance readability test.

## 16. Implementation roadmap

### Phase 0 — Animation and spatial truth

Current priority: fixed sprite canvases, shared ground pivots, consistent pose scale, stable dog anatomy, action-specific interaction anchors, a grounded fire bed, and visual regression captures.

Exit condition: no visible growth, sliding, floating, clipping, or off-target work actions during a long observation.

### Phase 1 — First causal resource slice

Build only three interactive nature assets: one foreground tree with dead-branch and felling states, one berry bush with seasonal and harvested states, and one flint/pyrite source. Add identities, discovery, anchors, depletion, and visible carried outputs.

Exit condition: the player can watch a person discover each source, approach it correctly, gather something visible, carry it home, and place it physically.

### Phase 2 — Day One origin

Remove pre-existing camp props from a fresh save; stage arrival and site assessment; create the first tinder nest, fire, and shelter from sourced materials; persist intermediate states; preserve calm fallbacks when attempts fail.

Exit condition: a fresh world produces a believable founding story with no unexplained asset appearance.

### Phase 3 — Material transformation and provenance

Add the transformation graph, physical staging instead of abstract inventory, tool condition and repair, interrupted-work persistence, and visible object lineage. The first complete chain is tree → pole → shelter or drying-rack support.

Exit condition: developer inspection can reveal complete lineage while the player understands it visually without inspection UI.

### Phase 4 — Story planner variation

Add contextual intention scoring, personal knowledge, cooperative invitations, memory, repetition suppression, failure, recovery, unfinished-work resumption, and quiet-period scheduling.

Exit condition: multiple multi-day runs produce recognizably different but plausible histories from the same landscape.

### Phase 5 — Exploration and known-world growth

Add neighbouring places, routes, path traces, discovery cues, knowledge return, expanding camera bounds, and world-strip/place-view transitions. The first new place is a berry grove or river bend close enough to keep the camp readable.

Exit condition: the world becomes physically larger because of an observed exploration story, without a map unlock or pop-in.

### Phase 6 — Hunt and hide lifecycle

Add animal signs, preparation, uncertainty, spatially honest return or processing, carcass materials, pole and cordage requirements, staged rack construction, and hide processing.

Exit condition: the hide rack visibly embodies every prior step and never appears from an abstract resource total.

### Phase 7 — Multiple lived places

Add place identity, habitual use, continuous zoom across scales, distant simulation visualisation, and the later-settlement location framework.

Exit condition: attention can move between places without becoming a conventional map or management UI.

### Phase 8 — Settlement and age framework

Add gradual density, path formation, building lifecycles, material reuse, knowledge transmission, generations, cultural memory, era transitions, and continuity into future villages and towns.

This phase begins only after the single Neolithic camp remains compelling for extended observation.

## 17. Recommended next vertical slice

Keep the next slice deliberately small.

### World assets

- one nearby foreground pine or birch
- one berry bush at the edge of the initial frame
- one flint/pyrite source
- one loose dry-grass patch
- one physical staging patch at camp

### Behaviours

- notice
- inspect
- gather by hand
- carry visibly
- place visibly
- prepare tinder
- strike and blow at the correct fire anchor
- return later because a source is remembered

### Stories this slice can produce

- Milo notices the berry bush before Nora.
- Otto returns from the flint source while Nora prepares tinder.
- Rain wets the first bundle and forces it under shelter.
- Nora teaches Otto which grass stayed dry.
- A dead branch is gathered before anyone considers felling a tree.
- The first successful fire creates a shared resting moment.

This proves the complete grammar—discovery, knowledge, material, transport, transformation, consequence—without hunting, economy, town systems, or later ages.

## 18. Acceptance tests

### Visual truth

- Feet and paws remain grounded in every pose.
- Body scale remains consistent across state changes.
- Hands, tools, materials, and objects meet at authored anchors.
- A carried object never teleports between source and destination.
- Construction stages share one footprint and grow physically.

### Causal truth

- No significant object exists without a creation or discovery event.
- No output exists without its source and transformation.
- Interrupted work remains in the world.
- A depleted source visibly changes.
- A discovered location is known only to appropriate characters until knowledge spreads.

### Story variation

- Ten simulated beginnings do not produce the same exact action order.
- Every beginning remains plausible and calm.
- Repeated actions differ through context rather than decorative text.
- Quiet observation periods occur naturally.

### Desktop-companion identity

- No permanent counters or task list are required.
- The scene remains understandable in a brief glance.
- The world can be ignored safely.
- Camera changes do not demand attention.
- Progress increases affection and memory, not pressure.

## 19. Scope guardrails

For the immediate milestone, do not build a player-facing inventory, resource counters, production queues, selectable worker jobs, technology trees, conventional fog of war, quest markers, building placement, combat, or later-age assets.

Internal state, provenance, and planning systems are allowed only insofar as they produce a more legible, living first camp.

## Final design test

For every new asset or feature, ask:

1. Where did it physically come from?
2. Who knows how to make or use it?
3. What did the player have a chance to observe?
4. What trace remains afterward?
5. Can the same ingredients produce a different plausible story next time?
6. Does this deepen the quiet life of the world without asking for attention?

If those questions do not have concrete answers, the feature is not ready to enter IdleCommand.
